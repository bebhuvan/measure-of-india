What is measured

The index does not follow prices. It follows one shopping list, and asks what that same list costs today.

Price collectors visit the same shops on the same days and record the price of the same items — 299 in villages, 299 in towns. The list does not change when households change what they buy, because if it did you could no longer tell a price rise from a change in habit.

What newspapers call inflation is not the index. It is the change in the index against the same month a year earlier. When inflation falls to 3.8%, nothing has become cheaper: the list costs 3.8% more than it did last June, instead of the 5.1% more it cost the June before.

Two indices are built, not one. Rural and urban India have separate baskets, separate weights and separate collection networks, and the two diverge for months at a time — the combined figure is a weighted average of them, and it can be quiet while one half of the country is not.

The index is set to 100 in 2012. A reading of 196.4 means the basket costs 96% more than it did then. It is not a price, and comparing index levels between countries or between base years tells you nothing at all.

Revision slip · overprinted in magenta

In January 2015 the base moved from 2010 to 2012 and the basket was rebuilt with new weights and new items. Values before and after are two different measurements wearing the same name. Do not splice them into one line — the Atlas keeps them as separate series, which is why this plate begins in 2015.

Reading the plate

Common error

Falling inflation is not falling prices

It means prices are climbing more slowly. Prices only fall when the rate goes below zero — rare for the headline, and last seen for food in late 2025.

Common error

Don’t blame the month you are reading

A year-on-year figure has two ends. A soft reading can come entirely from an unusually high price twelve months ago — a base effect — while nothing unusual happened this month.

Better question

Whose basket is it?

Food is 45.9% of the national basket. If food is a smaller share of your spending, your lived inflation is not this line, and the gap is not an error in either.

Better question

What moved, not how much

A 3.8% headline built on 8% vegetables and 1% everything else describes a different economy from a broad, even 3.8%. The sub-indices carry the story.

What this plate cannot tell you

The weights are from the Consumer Expenditure Survey of 2011–12. Every decade since Independence, Indian households have spent a smaller share of income on food. A basket fixed in 2011 almost certainly gives food more weight than it deserves in 2026, which means the headline is more sensitive to a bad monsoon than the country now is.

A weight revision has been pending for more than a decade. This is the single largest known limitation of the series, and no adjustment in this plate corrects for it. Where the Atlas cannot correct something, it says so rather than quietly smoothing it away.
