Inflation ends the year at its calmest since 2019

Retail prices rose 3.5% in December — a third straight month below the RBI's 4% target. Food, which drove every spike of the past decade, is finally quiet. The question for 2026 is whether the quiet survives the monsoon.

New Delhi, Jan 12The Consumer Price Index closed 2025 the way it has spent most of it: quietly. December's print of – keeps headline inflation below the midpoint of the Reserve Bank's tolerance band for a third consecutive month, a run last seen in the deflation-scare winter of 2018–19.

The calm is imported from the vegetable mandi. Food and beverage inflation halved over the course of 2025, from a peak above ten per cent last winter to – in December. Because food carries nearly half the weight of the basket, its quiet overwhelms almost everything else the index has to say.

Everything else, on inspection, is saying something mildly uncomfortable. The core index — the basket minus food and fuel — has been pinned near – for twelve months. That is the portion of inflation set by domestic demand, wages and rents, and it is the portion interest rates can reach. It is not falling.

Fuel, a small weight with a loud political life, has emerged from a year of statistical deflation caused by 2023's LPG price cuts washing through the comparison. It now sits at –, contributing nothing in either direction. The RBI's next statement is due in February; this print gives it room, and no urgency.

How to read this · Standing note

All four lines answer the same question — how much dearer than twelve months ago? The red headline is a weighted average and must therefore live between its parts: food drags it, core anchors it. The grey band is law, not preference: since 2016 the RBI must explain itself to government whenever the red line spends three quarters outside it.

The current reading · This month

2025 is only the second sustained excursion into the lower half of the band in a decade. The shape to watch is the wedge between core and headline: while core sits above the red line, the average is being flattered by vegetables, and the flattery ends with the next bad monsoon.

Caution

How to misread this — six reliable methods

  1. “Inflation is down, so prices are down.”

    Falling inflation is prices rising more slowly. At 3.5%, the basket still costs 3.5% more than last December. Only a negative print means falling prices — food has managed it twice in ten years.

  2. Forgetting the base.

    Every y/y figure is a ratio to the same month last year. A spike then flatters now; a collapse then haunts now. Before reacting to any print, ask what the denominator was doing.

  3. Calling core the “real” inflation.

    Core excludes what poor households spend most on. It is watched because it is stable and policy-responsive, not because it is truer. The headline is the honest cost of living.

  4. Mistaking the basket for your basket.

    Weights come from a 2011–12 survey. They understate urban rent, overstate cereals for the well-off, and describe nobody precisely. The CPI averages 1.4 billion private inflations.

  5. Reading one month as a trend.

    Vegetables can move the headline by a full point in a single month and reverse the next. Three consecutive months is the minimum unit of meaning; twelve is better.

  6. Pitting CPI against WPI.

    Consumer retail prices versus factory-gate prices: different baskets, different purposes, often five points apart. Both correct. Show them side by side, never as a contradiction to resolve.
