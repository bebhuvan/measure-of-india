Prices, and the nine ways India measures them

No single number tells you what is happening to prices in India. Nine do, they disagree with each other, and the disagreements are the point. This dossier puts all nine on one time axis, then opens up the one most people mean when they say inflation — the Consumer Price Index — down to the six groups that move it.

The family  ·  nine measures of the same thing

One shared time axis  ·  Jan 2015 — Jun 2026
Read down the column: every row is drawn on the same eleven years, so the shapes are directly comparable even though the vertical scales are not. Wholesale prices swing four times as hard as shop prices. The repo rate is the only row that was decided rather than measured. Where a row belongs to another plate, the reference is printed beside its tag.

Each row carries its own vertical range, printed under its name; the figure at the right is the latest reading. Rows cross zero where a dotted line is shown. The GDP deflator is quarterly, which is why its line is visibly coarser — the Atlas does not resample a series to make it look like its neighbours.

Nine rows, four kinds of measure: the headline and its splits, its components, two rival measures built on different baskets, and one policy rate that responds to all of them.

Plate II  ·  The six groups, on the same axis

Own vertical scale per row  ·  shared time axis
Weights

What is in the basket

Each cell is one per cent of household spending. Food alone carries 46 of them, which is why View C is mostly blue.

Each row has its own vertical scale — printed under its name — because a group that swings between −5% and +14% cannot share an axis with one that never leaves 2–6%. Only the time axis is shared. Read the rows for shape; read the two figures at the right for level and for what each group contributed this month.

What the index is

The list does not change when households change what they buy. That is deliberate: if the basket moved every month you could no longer tell a price rise from a change in habit. The cost of holding it still is that it ages — and India’s has been held still an unusually long time.

Common error

Falling inflation is not falling prices. It means prices are climbing more slowly. Prices only fall when the rate goes below zero, as food did in late 2025 and the headline almost never does.

What the headlines call inflation is not the index. It is the change in the index against the same month a year earlier. When inflation falls to 3.8%, nothing has become cheaper. The list still costs more than it did last June — 3.8% more, instead of the 5.1% more it cost the June before.

Common error

196.4 is not rupees and not a price. Index levels can be compared with nothing except their own past — never across countries, never across base years.

The index itself stands at 196.4 against 100 in 2012, which says the 2012 basket costs 96% more now than it did then. Two such indices are built, not one: rural and urban India have separate baskets, separate weights and separate collection networks, and View B is simply the distance between them.

How to read the plate

Common error

Don’t blame the month you are reading. A year-on-year figure has two ends: a soft reading can come entirely from an unusually high price twelve months ago.

Start with View C, not View A. A 3.8% headline built on food alone is a different economy from a broad, even 3.8% — and the stack tells you which it is. Through 2022 the green and red bands do real work; by 2026 the headline is held up almost entirely by blue and gold, which is to say by food and by services.

Bands that drop below the zero line are groups that subtracted from the headline. Fuel spent most of 2023 and 2024 down there, which is why the headline eased in those years while almost nothing in a household budget felt cheaper.

Better question

Whose basket is it? If food is a smaller share of your spending than 45.9%, your lived inflation is not View A — and the gap between the two is not an error in either.

Then read View B. Rural and urban inflation diverge for months at a time, and the combined line can be perfectly calm while one half of the country is not. The shaded space is the part of the story the headline averages away.

What this plate cannot tell you

Known limitation

Weights are frozen at the 2011–12 Consumer Expenditure Survey. A revision has been pending for more than ten years.

Every decade since Independence, Indian households have spent a smaller share of their money on food. A basket built in 2011 almost certainly gives food more weight than it deserves in 2026, which makes the headline more sensitive to a bad monsoon than the country now is. This is the single largest known limitation of the series, and nothing on this plate corrects for it.

Where the Atlas cannot correct something, it marks it and says so. That is what View D is for.

Base year
The year set to 100. India uses 2012. Changing it re-scales every value and breaks comparison across the join.
Base effect
When this month’s rate moves because of what happened a year ago rather than anything happening now.
Weight
The share of household spending a group carries. Food holds 45.86 of every 100 rupees.
Contribution
Weight times a group’s own inflation — what that group added to the headline, in percentage points.
Core inflation
The headline with food and fuel removed, because those two swing hardest and fastest.
Series break
A point where method changed enough that values on either side are not comparable.
