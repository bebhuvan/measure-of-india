What it does not say

It does not say prices are falling — the basket is still – dearer than a year ago. And it does not say underlying pressure has eased: core inflation, computed at –, has been flat for a year and now sits above the headline.

When core exceeds headline, the average is being held down by volatile items. That configuration is stable only until the next weather shock. We do not know which way 2026 breaks; the next two prints will say.

What this measures

The Consumer Price Index tracks the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services — rice, rent, bus fares, haircuts, school fees — as an urban or rural household actually buys them. When we say "inflation is 3.5 per cent", we mean this basket costs 3.5 per cent more than it did a year ago.

The basket is not a guess. It comes from a massive survey of what households actually spent in 2011–12, and each item carries a weight proportional to its share of household budgets. Food weighs about 46 per cent; housing, 10; fuel, 7.

It is a Laspeyres index — the basket is fixed, so it measures pure price change. That fixedness is its strength and its blind spot: the number stays cleanly comparable, but slowly drifts from how India actually lives, until the base is revised.

Margin note · i Falling inflation ≠ falling prices. The price level almost never returns. A falling rate is slower pain, not relief.
Margin note · ii Watch the base. A high print last year flatters this year's number with nothing improving. Ask: compared to what?
Margin note · iii Core is derived, not published. The NSO releases no official core index; ours is computed from group indices and labelled as such.

How to misread this number

  • i.

    "Inflation fell, so prices fell."

    No. Falling inflation means prices are rising more slowly — disinflation. At 3.5%, the basket still costs 3.5% more than last December. Only a negative print means falling prices, and food has managed that exactly twice in a decade.

  • ii.

    "My inflation is the national inflation."

    Your basket is not the average basket. The weights are a 2011–12 snapshot; they understate urban rent, overstate cereals for the well-off, and describe nobody precisely. A family spending 60 per cent on food lives in a different index entirely.

  • iii.

    "One month tells a story."

    Vegetables can move the headline by a full point in a single month and reverse the next. Month-to-month moves are mostly seasonal noise; three consecutive months is the minimum unit of meaning, and twelve is better.

  • iv.

    "The base doesn't matter."

    Every y/y figure is a ratio to the same month a year ago. A spike then flatters now; a collapse then haunts now. Before reacting to any print, ask what the denominator was doing.

  • v.

    "Core is the real inflation."

    Core excludes what poor households spend most on. It is watched because it is stable and policy-responsive, not because it is truer. For the cost of living as lived, the headline is the honest number.

  • vi.

    "CPI and WPI contradict each other."

    They survey different terrain — retail consumers versus the factory gate, with different baskets and different fuels. Divergence of five points is normal. Both are correct; show them as separate series, never as a conflict to resolve.

Revisions & uncertainty

CPI is final on release — it is never revised, which makes it unusual among Indian statistics. Where any source restates a figure, both vintages are shown as separate series.

Where something is not measured, this dossier says "we do not know" rather than inventing a number.
