The speed of the whole economy.

Four times a year, the statisticians add up everything India produces — every factory, farm, office and street cart, in principle — and tell us how fast it is growing. The answer is one number built from a hundred arguments.

What the print says

The economy grew – in the September quarter, accelerating from – in the previous one. The composition is the encouraging part: household consumption — fifty-six per cent of GDP — rose –, and investment held at –, so the quarter was carried by domestic demand rather than statistical residue.

GVA, which strips out taxes and subsidies, came in slightly lower at – — the gap is a net-tax story, not a production story.

What it does not say

It does not say the average Indian is 6.9% better off — GDP says nothing about distribution, and per-capita growth is lower once population is counted. It says nothing reliable about the informal economy, which is estimated, not measured.

And unlike the CPI, this number will be revised — this is the second estimate of this quarter, and the final figure, due in about three years, may look different. We do not yet know how different.

What this measures

Gross Domestic Product is the market value of everything produced inside India's borders in a period, with double-counting removed. It can be added up three ways — by what is produced, by what is spent, and by what is earned — and in India the three never quite agree, so the accounts publish a "discrepancies" line to absorb the difference.

The headline is real GDP: values at constant 2011–12 prices, so that growth means more output, not just higher prices. The nominal figure — current prices — runs several points higher when inflation is high, and it is the nominal number that tax collections and debt ratios actually care about.

Unlike the CPI, which is final on release, the national accounts are revised relentlessly: a quarter's growth is first estimated two months after it ends, then restated as annual surveys, tax data and the new base year arrive. Today's 6.9 is a pencil sketch that hardens over three years.

Margin note · i The rebound is arithmetic. +20% after −24% does not restore the level: 0.76 × 1.20 ≈ 0.91. The economy was still ~9% smaller.
Margin note · ii Real vs nominal. Real growth measures volume; nominal measures rupees. Debt-to-GDP and tax buoyancy live in the nominal world.
Margin note · iii GDP ≠ GVA. GDP adds net indirect taxes to GVA. When subsidy payments swing, the two diverge — check which one a headline is quoting.

How to misread this number

  • i.

    "+20% means a boom."

    After a −24% collapse, +20% leaves the level about 9% below where it started. Growth rates multiply, they do not add. Always ask what the base quarter was.

  • ii.

    "GDP growth is my growth."

    GDP says nothing about distribution or headcount. Per-capita growth is lower; median experience can be lower still. An aggregate can rise while most households tread water.

  • iii.

    "The first estimate is the fact."

    Quarterly GDP is revised for years as better data arrives — sometimes by more than a point. The CPI is final; GDP is a draft. Quote the vintage, not just the number.

  • iv.

    "Real and nominal are interchangeable."

    Real growth strips out price change; nominal does not. With 5% inflation, 7% real growth is 12% nominal — and it is the nominal figure that swells tax receipts and shrinks debt ratios.

  • v.

    "GDP and GVA disagree."

    They differ by net indirect taxes, nothing more. A GDP–GVA gap is an accounting story about taxes and subsidies, not evidence of statistical confusion.

  • vi.

    "Quarterly momentum = y/y growth."

    India publishes year-on-year quarterly growth; many countries publish annualised quarter-on-quarter. The two can move in opposite directions. Never compare India's 6.9 with America's 2.8 without checking which convention each used.

Revisions & uncertainty

National accounts are revised continuously: quarterly estimates are restated with each new release and finalised over roughly three years. This dossier quotes vintages explicitly; where estimates conflict, both are shown.

Where something is not measured, this dossier says "we do not know" rather than inventing a number.
